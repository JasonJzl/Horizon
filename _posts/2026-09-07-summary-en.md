### [LLM writing without disclosure is intellectual dishonesty](https://bcantrill.dtrace.org/2025/12/05/your-intellectual-fly-is-open/) ⭐️ 7.0/10

In his December 2025 essay &quot;Your intellectual fly is open \(2025\)&quot;, Bryan Cantrill argues that using LLMs to write without disclosure undermines intellectual authenticity, because writing is a form of thinking and the LLM is not the author. He contends that LLMs also remain poor writers and, most importantly, are not the person whose name is attached to the piece. In his view, undisclosed LLM authorship therefore misrepresents the provenance and reasoning behind published text. The post is an opinion piece rather than a technical analysis, framing disclosure as a matter of intellectual honesty for writers who use AI tools.

hackernews · cyb0rg0 · Sep 6, 11:56 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49585644)

**「Background」** Bryan Cantrill is a well-known systems engineer, co-creator of DTrace, and long-time technology essayist blogging at The Observation Deck on bcantrill.dtrace.org. In this December 2025 post, he uses the metaphor of an open zipper \(&\#x27;your intellectual fly is open&\#x27;\) to argue that undisclosed AI-assisted writing is a visible tell that undermines a writer&\#x27;s credibility, because writing itself is a form of thinking. The post specifically reacts to platforms like LinkedIn encouraging users to &\#x27;rewrite it with AI,&\#x27; warning that such text often reads poorly and reveals that the author did not actually think through the words.

**「Community discussion」** Commenters largely agreed that authenticity matters: jeremyjh stressed that writing changes and clarifies the writer’s own thinking, while jgrahamc drew on his Cloudflare editing experience to say that preserving an individual writing voice is nearly as important as the content itself. Another commenter, dynm, questioned whether the objection should rest on LLMs being bad writers now, asking whether critics would still object if AI prose became indistinguishable from human writing.

### [DNS and New gTLDs: One in Five Registrations May Be Scams](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Sep/6/the-purpose-of-dns-is-to-spread-scams/) ⭐️ 7.0/10

Simon Willison highlighted Terence Eden&\#x27;s analysis of an Interisle report on DNS abuse, which found that approximately 85 million new gTLD registrations were made in 2025 and 8.5 million had been added to blocklists by May 2025. The report estimates that a 10% abuse rate is likely the floor, with the real figure probably closer to 20%, meaning as many as one in five newly registered gTLD domains may be used for scams. Eden argues that the Domain Name System has effectively become a vector for criminals to run scams at a very high rate, and notes that ICANN has discussed this issue for years. The post underscores the scale of domain abuse despite ongoing industry awareness and policy discussion.

rss · Simon Willison · Sep 6, 14:40

**「Background」** Generic top-level domains \(gTLDs\) are domain name categories administered by ICANN, and their expansion has made domains easier to register for legitimate and malicious purposes alike. Blocklists are commonly used to identify domains involved in phishing, malware, and scams, so their presence provides a measurable indicator of DNS abuse across new registrations.

**「Impact」** For registrars, security teams, and internet users, the report implies that a substantial share of new gTLD registrations may be abusive, increasing the need for faster detection and takedown mechanisms even as ICANN continues to debate how to handle domain abuse.

### [Why Greenfield Rewrites Usually Fail: Simon Willison&\#x27;s Take](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Sep/6/theres-no-limit-to-how-bad-code-can-get/) ⭐️ 6.0/10

In a Lobste.rs comment, Simon Willison argues that rewriting a legacy system from scratch rarely succeeds: the old system keeps changing because it runs core business, its maintainers invest minimal effort as they expect it to be replaced, and the greenfield team lacks a full understanding of the behavior and scope they must reproduce. After months or years without delivering value, pressure to ship usually produces a new system covering only a subset of features, leaving two production systems and risking abandonment. Willison points to Will Larson&\#x27;s &\#x27;Migrations: the sole scalable fix to tech debt&\#x27; and recommends instead shoring up the old system with automated tests and targeted refactors.

rss · Simon Willison · Sep 6, 09:08

**「Background」** Technical debt refers to the long-term cost of quick-and-dirty code choices that make future changes slower. When debt becomes overwhelming, some teams propose a &\#x27;greenfield&\#x27; rewrite—building a new system from scratch—rather than gradually improving the existing one. This piece explains why that strategy often backfires in practice.
